
\section{Consequence of Credibility}\label{sup:thm}

\begin{thm}
  \label{Thm:bcc}
  If the likelihood is correctly specified according to the true data generating mechanism $p(\y | \bt)$, then a $1-\alpha$ credible set for any parameter $\theta_j$ will satisfy $\int \cvr(\theta_j)p(\bt) d\bt = 1 - \alpha$.
\end{thm}

\begin{proof}
By definition, a $100(1-\alpha)\%$ credible region for $\theta_j$ is any set $\cA_j(\y)$ such that $\int I\{\theta_j \in \cA_j(\y)\} p(\bt|\y)\,d\bt = 1 - \alpha$. The coverage probability, meanwhile, is defined as $\int I\{\theta_j \in \cA_j(\y)\} p(\y | \bt)d\y$. The average coverage, integrated with respect to the prior $p(\bt)$, is therefore

\as{
\int \int I\{\theta_j \in \cA_j(\y)\} p(\y | \bt) p(\bt) d\y d\bt
&=  \int \int I\{\theta_j \in \cA_j(\y)\} p(\bt | \y) p(\y) d\y d\bt \\
&=  \int \int I\{\theta_j \in \cA_j(\y)\} p(\bt | \y) d\bt p(\y) d\y \\
&=  \int  (1 - \alpha) p(\y) d\y \\
&=  1 - \alpha,
}

\noindent and as a result the average coverage is equal to the nominal coverage.
\end{proof}

The above theorem concerns a single parameter of interest $\theta_j$ and its credible interval. An immediate corollary of this result is that the average coverage of $p$ such intervals will also be equal to $1-\alpha$.

\section{Computing Quantiles from the Conditional Posterior}\label{sup:A}

Let $\y$ be a length $n$ vector of independent outcomes and $\X$ be an $n \times p$ matrix of features. Here we assume $\X$ has been standardized s.t. $\x_j\Tr\x_j = n$.  Let $\hat{S} = \lbrace k: \bh_k \neq  0 \rbrace$ denote the set of selected features. Then, let $\hat{S}_j$ denote the set of selected features that excludes feature $j$, so that $\hat{S}_j = \hat{S} \text{ if } j \notin \hat{S}$ and $\hat{S}_j = \hat{S} - \lbrace j \rbrace \text{ if } j \in \hat{S}$. Define $\Q_{\hat{S}_j}$ as $\I - \X_{\hat{S}_j}(\X_{\hat{S}_j}\Tr \X_{\hat{S}_j})^{-1} \X_{\hat{S}_j}\Tr$, the projection matrix onto the features selected by the lasso. The PIPE likelihood for $\beta_j$ conditional on the selected features is
\as{
  \begin{aligned}
  L(\beta_j|\hat{S}_j) &\propto \exp(-\tfrac{\tilde{n}}{2\sigma^2}(\beta_{j} - z_j)^2), \\
  \end{aligned}
}

\noindent where $z_j = (\x_j\Tr \Q_{\hat{S}_j} \x_j)^{-1} \x_j\Tr \Q_{\hat{S}_j} \y$ and $\tilde{n} = \x_j\Tr \Q_{\hat{S}_j} \x_j$.

The lasso can be formulated as a Bayesian regression model with a Laplace (double exponential) prior. In this case, the prior for $\beta_j$ is proportional to $\exp(-\frac{\tilde{n} \lambda} {\sigma^2} \abs{\beta_j})$. This prior keeps the value of $\lam$ consistent with the estimation procedure.

With this the form of the conditional posterior can be worked out as follows:
\as{
p(\beta_j | \hat{S}_j) &\propto \exp(-\tfrac{\tilde{n}}{2\sigma^2} (\beta_j^2 - 2z_j\beta_j)) \exp(-\tfrac{\tilde{n} \lambda} {\sigma^2} \abs{\beta_j}) \\
&= \exp(-\tfrac{\tilde{n}}{2\sigma^2} (\beta_j^2 - 2 z_j\beta_j +  2 \lambda \abs{\beta_j})) \\
&= \exp(-\tfrac{\tilde{n}}{2\sigma^2} (\beta_j^2 - 2(z_j\beta_j - \lambda \abs{\beta_j}))) \\
&=
\begin{cases}
\exp(-\frac{\tilde{n}}{2\sigma^2} (\beta_j^2 - 2(z_j + \lambda)\beta_j)), \text{ if } \beta_j < 0, \\
\exp(-\frac{\tilde{n}}{2\sigma^2} (\beta_j^2 - 2(z_j - \lambda)\beta_j )), \text{ if } \beta_j \geq 0 \\
\end{cases} \\
&\propto
\begin{cases}
C_{1} \exp\{-\frac{\tilde{n}}{2\sigma^2} (\beta_j - (z_j + \lambda))^2\}, \text{ if } \beta_j < 0, \\
C_{2} \exp\{-\frac{\tilde{n}}{2\sigma^2} (\beta_j - (z_j - \lambda))^2\}, \text{ if } \beta_j \geq 0 \\
\end{cases}
}
where $C_{1} = \exp(z_j \lambda \tilde{n}/\sigma^2)$ and $C_{2} = \exp(-z_j \lambda \tilde{n}/\sigma^2)$. The piecewise distribution is made up of two scaled truncated Normal distributions. 

The general idea to compute the quantiles is to map $p$ corresponding to a quantile of interest $q$ to a corresponding $p_*$ on one of the underlying (unscaled) Normal distributions ($\Norm(z_j \pm \lam, \sigma^2 / \tilde{n})$) where the CDF can be inverted to compute the quantile $q$.

While the process will be described on the standard scale, in practice all computations occur on the log scale. Assume $\hat{\sigma}^2$ has been estimated. Let $i = 1$ refer to the left half of the Laplace-Normal and $i = 2$ refer to the right half. Then let $I_i = [\ell_i, u_i]$ be the support for the respective half, that is $I_1=[-\infty,0)$ and $I_2=[0,\infty]$. Let $\Phi_i(x)$ and $\Phi_i^{-1}(x)$ be the CDF and its inverse for the $i^{th}$ underlying Normal, again that is $\Norm(z_j \pm \lambda, \hat{\sigma}^2 / \tilde{n})$. On their respective support, let the total probability in the underlying unscaled Normals be $U_i = \Phi_i(u_i) - \Phi_i(\ell_i)$. Additionally let the probability in the left and right halves of the Laplace-Normal be denoted $P_i$. $P_i$ can be computed stably as follows. Let $r_i = \log(C_iU_i)$ and $m = \max\{r_1, r_2\}$, then $c = m + \log(\exp(r_1 - m) + \exp(r_2 - m))$ and $P_i = \exp(r_i - c)$. Note $P_1 + P_2 = 1$. Using $P_i$, we can determine which half $q$ comes from. If $p < P_1$ then the quantile comes from the left otherwise it comes from the right.

Then, the transformation to map $p$ to $p_*$ if $q$ comes from the left side of the Laplace-Normal is simply: 

$$
p_* = \frac{U_{1}}{P_{1}}p.
$$

The weight $U_{1} / P_{1}$ indicates how much probability in the underlying Normal transformed onto counts relative to the probability in the Laplace-Normal over support $I_1$. If the quantile is in the right side, the map is a bit more complicated:

$$
p_* = \frac{U_{2}}{P_{2}} (p - P_1) + \Phi_{2}(0).
$$

Again, the weight $U_{2} / P_{2}$ indicates how much probability in the underlying Normal transformed onto counts relative to the probability in the Laplace-Normal over the respective support. $P_1$ tells how much probability has already been accounted for in the left half of the Laplace-Normal. Together, these tell how much probability is needed in the Normal being transformed onto to get to the quantile. However, we need to accumulate this probability from $0$, so $\Phi_{2}(0)$ tells us how far we need to shift over to start at $0$. 

Once $p_*$ has been computed, then $q = \Phi_1^{-1}(p_*)$ if the quantile comes from the left side and $q = \Phi_2^{-1}(p_*)$ if it comes from the right.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Compute Quantile for RL-P Laplace-Normal Distribution}
\label{alg:quantile}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \Require
    $z_j,\;\hat{\sigma}^2,\;\tilde{n},\lambda,\;p$ \Comment{mean, variance, effective sample size, penalty, probability of interest}
  \Statex
  \State // 1. Compute total log probability in left/right Normals over respective supports
  \State $U_{1} \gets \log \left(\Phi\bigl(0;\,z_j + \lambda,\;\tfrac{\hat{\sigma}^2}{\tilde{n}}\bigr)\right)$
  \State $U_{2} \gets \log \left(1 - \Phi\bigl(0;\,z_j - \lambda,\;\tfrac{\hat{\sigma}^2}{\tilde{n}}\bigr)\right)$
  \Statex
  \State // 2. Compute $P_{1}, P_{2}$
  \State $r_{1} \gets \log C_{1} + U_{1}$
  \State $r_{2} \gets \log C_{2} + U_{2}$
  \State $m \gets \max \lbrace r_{1}, r_{2} \rbrace$
  \State $c \gets m + \log\left(\exp(r_{1} - m) + \exp(r_{2} - m)\right)$
  \State $P_{1} \gets \exp(r_{1} - c)$
  \State $P_{2} \gets 1 - P_{1}$
  \Statex
  \State // 3. Invert CDF on the appropriate unscaled Normal
  \If{$p \le P_{1}$}
    \State $w \gets \dfrac{U_{1}}{P_{1}}$
    \State $q \gets \Phi^{-1}\bigl(p \,w;\;z_j + \lambda,\;\tfrac{\hat{\sigma}^2}{n}\bigr)$
  \Else
    \State $w \gets \dfrac{U_{2}}{P_{2}}$
    \State $s \gets \Phi(0; z_j - \lambda, \tfrac{\hat{\sigma}^2}{n})$
    \State $q \gets \Phi^{-1}\!\Bigl(w(p - P_{1}) + s;z_j - \lambda,\;\tfrac{\hat{\sigma}^2}{n}\Bigr)$
  \EndIf
  \State \Return $q$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Coverage Behavior Under Alternative Sample Sizes}\label{sup:alt_ns}

Figure~\ref{fig:C1} displays coverage estimates as a smooth function of $\beta$ for three values of n: 50, 100, and 400 but otherwise uses the same setup as the simulation described in Section~\ref{Sec:coverage}.

\begin{figure}[hbtp]
  \begin{center}
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\linewidth]{figureC1}
  \caption{\label{fig:C1} Coverage for PIPE-P intervals by sample size, computed using the simulation in Section~\ref{Sec:coverage} where for each sample size, 1000 datasets were generated with coefficients $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ set to $p = 101$ equally spaced quantiles of a Laplace distribution (shaded distribution). The curved lines represent smoothed fits of the empirical coverages obtained using binomial GAMs. The dashed lines are the average coverage rates for all intervals constructed on the $1000$ data sets. The solid black line indicates the 80\% nominal coverage rate.}
  \end{center}
\end{figure}

For $n = 50$, coverage is overconservative with the characteristic high coverage for values near zero and low coverage for larger values of $\beta$. However, As $n$ is increased to 100 then 400 the characteristic over coverage for small $\beta$ values and under coverage for large $\beta$ values lessens, largely attributable to $\lam_{\CV}$ being smaller.

The results here provide an example of a version of the PIPE-P method but using an MCP penalty instead of a lasso penalty to obtain intervals. The MCP penalty closely resembles that of the lasso near zero but eventually levels out to a constant penalty for larger values of $\beta$, unlike the lasso which applies a penalty proportional to the magnitude of $\beta$. Note that although $\beta$s with intermediate magnitudes are still under covered, that the largest $\beta$s have coverage over 60\%, almost doubling the coverage of the lasso based PIPE-P intervals.
